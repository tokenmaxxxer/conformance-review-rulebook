# Design and User Research Roles in Software Product Development

## Roles

- **UX Researcher**: focuses on understanding user behaviors and needs through early-stage, data-driven research that feeds into design decisions ([Springboard](https://www.springboard.com/blog/design/ux-researcher-vs-ux-designer/), [Lyssna](https://www.lyssna.com/blog/user-experience-research-vs-user-experience-design/)).
- **UX Designer**: translates research insights into usable, satisfying interactions; typically owns usability testing, interaction flows, and user-friendliness within the design phase ([Designlab](https://designlab.com/blog/product-designer-vs-ux-designer-understanding-the-key-differences), [Door3](https://www.door3.com/blog/ux-researcher-vs-ux-designer-understanding-the-differences)).
- **UI Designer**: implied in the same sources as the visual/surface layer distinct from UX (composition, visuals) — treated as narrower than UX design, though sources searched define it mainly by contrast rather than as a fully independent job description [unsourced: no dedicated primary source retrieved on UI-vs-UX boundary beyond incidental mentions].
- **Product Designer**: broader, business-oriented role spanning the full product lifecycle (not just pre-launch), combining business strategy, project management, and UX craft; manages/refines a product post-launch, whereas UX designers are more associated with pre-market development stages ([UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/product-designer-vs-ux-designer-a-comparative-analysis/), [Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/articles/product-designer-vs-ux-designer-the-difference-explained), [LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/ux-design/product-designer-vs-ux-designer/)).
- **Interaction Designer**: not separately sourced in this pass [unsourced].
- **Design Systems Engineer**: a hybrid frontend-engineering + design role that builds and maintains the shared UI foundation (reusable components, tokens, patterns, documentation) enabling consistent, accessible delivery across product teams — explicitly a distinct engineering-leaning job, not a renamed designer role ([DevOpsSchool](https://www.devopsschool.com/blog/design-systems-engineer-role-blueprint-responsibilities-skills-kpis-and-career-path/)).
- **Content Designer / UX Writer**: broadly the same role under two names; both own copy, microcopy, content hierarchy, voice/tone. In smaller teams one person does both; at scale, orgs often still don't split them into separate specialties — "content designer" and "UX writer" are largely interchangeable titles for the same function ([Working In Content](https://workingincontent.com/resources/compare-content-design-to-ux-design), [UX Writing Hub](https://uxwritinghub.com/ux-writing-vs-content-design/)).
- **Design Ops**: a distinct operational/program-management function (not a design-craft role) — orchestrates people, process, and tools so designers/researchers can focus on the work; includes sub-roles like design/UX producers (project-level) and design program managers (org-level) ([NN/g DesignOps 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-operations-101/), [NN/g Who Does DesignOps](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designops-roles-partnerships/)).
- **ResearchOps**: a specialization within DesignOps, owning the operational aspects of user research specifically (recruiting, panels, tooling, repositories) ([NN/g Who Does DesignOps](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designops-roles-partnerships/)).
- **Service Designer**: not independently sourced in this research pass [unsourced].

### Which are genuinely distinct vs. renamed
- Distinct in practice, per sourced material: UX Researcher vs. UX Designer vs. Product Designer (different scope of lifecycle and business involvement) ([UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/product-designer-vs-ux-designer-a-comparative-analysis/)); Design Systems Engineer vs. Product/UX Designer (engineering vs. design craft) ([DevOpsSchool](https://www.devopsschool.com/blog/design-systems-engineer-role-blueprint-responsibilities-skills-kpis-and-career-path/)); DesignOps/ResearchOps vs. design/research practitioners (operations vs. craft) ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-operations-101/)).
- Same job, different label: Content Designer and UX Writer are described directly as "two different names for the same role" in most organizations, with true specialization only appearing at large scale ([Working In Content](https://workingincontent.com/resources/compare-content-design-to-ux-design)).

## Responsibility boundaries

- UX Researcher → produces insights and behavioral/attitudinal data that UX Designers consume to shape flows and interfaces; Product Designers additionally weigh business goals against those insights ([Springboard](https://www.springboard.com/blog/design/ux-researcher-vs-ux-designer/)).
- Product Designers own strategy and full lifecycle management (including post-launch iteration); UX Designers are more concentrated on the pre-launch design/build stage of a feature or product ([UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/product-designer-vs-ux-designer-a-comparative-analysis/)).
- Design Systems Engineers own the shared component/token layer used across product teams, sitting at the boundary between design and frontend engineering, and are consumed by both designers (who use the components in design files) and engineers (who implement from the same source) ([DevOpsSchool](https://www.devopsschool.com/blog/design-systems-engineer-role-blueprint-responsibilities-skills-kpis-and-career-path/)).
- DesignOps/ResearchOps do not produce design or research artifacts themselves; they own process, tooling, recruiting, and coordination so that designers/researchers can focus on craft work ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-operations-101/)).
- Content Designers/UX Writers own copy, microcopy, and content strategy; UX/Product Designers own composition and visual/interaction structure — separating these lets each specialist focus on their expertise, per industry commentary ([UX Writing Hub](https://uxwritinghub.com/ux-writers-vs-ux-designers/)).

## Artifacts produced

Per the research angle's list of artifacts (research plan, discussion guide, usability report, persona, journey map, service blueprint, wireframe, hi-fi mockup, prototype, design spec/redlines, design system component doc, accessibility audit): this research pass sourced strong material on usability testing/reports (via the NN/g 5-user debate) and design-system/component documentation (via the Design Systems Engineer role), and on developer-handoff specs (tokens, states, breakpoints — see Handoff section). Detailed per-artifact content/consumer breakdowns for research plan, discussion guide, persona, journey map, service blueprint, wireframe, hi-fi mockup, and prototype were **not directly retrieved in the sources gathered this pass** [unsourced — would require dedicated searches on each artifact type, e.g. NN/g's persona and journey-map guides].

- **Usability report**: output of usability testing; historically justified via Nielsen's "5 users is enough" claim for qualitative testing, with NN/g itself distinguishing that qualitative studies may need ~5 users per uniform group while quantitative studies need larger samples (NN/g's own newer guidance cites ~20 for quantitative, other sources cite up to 40) ([NN/g "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/), [NN/g "How Many Test Users"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-many-test-users/), [ResearchGate rebuttal](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200553185_Why_and_when_five_test_users_aren't_enough)). Consumed by designers and PMs to prioritize fixes.
- **Design system component documentation**: describes reusable components, patterns, and design tokens; built/maintained by Design Systems Engineers; consumed by both product designers (assembling screens) and frontend engineers (implementing code) ([DevOpsSchool](https://www.devopsschool.com/blog/design-systems-engineer-role-blueprint-responsibilities-skills-kpis-and-career-path/)).
- **Developer handoff spec** (Figma Dev Mode output): should define states (loading, empty, error, success), tokens, breakpoints, data assumptions, interaction rules, and accessibility requirements — best-practice guidance explicitly says missing these categories is a primary failure mode; consumed by engineers implementing the UI ([DEV Community summary of handoff study](https://dev.to/hunterstein/91-of-teams-say-design-handoff-is-broken-heres-how-to-fix-it-with-figma-to-azure-5h7), [Figma Dev Mode guide](https://www.figma.com/best-practices/guide-to-developer-handoff/)).

## Handoff points

1. **Designer → engineer (Figma Dev Mode) handoff is widely reported as broken**: a cited industry study found 92% of designers and 91% of developers believe there is room for improvement in the handoff process — near-universal dissatisfaction, not an edge case ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/hunterstein/91-of-teams-say-design-handoff-is-broken-heres-how-to-fix-it-with-figma-to-azure-5h7)).
2. **Root causes are process/tooling, not people**: version confusion (unclear "final" file, outdated links, unlabeled branches) and missing implementation details (states, tokens, breakpoints, data assumptions, interaction rules, accessibility requirements) are the two dominant, recurring failure patterns across roles; one source explicitly frames the friction as "mostly wasn't about people — it was about process, tooling, and structure" ([Quest Works](https://www.questworks.io/blog/design-engineering-handoff), [DEV Community](https://dev.to/hunterstein/91-of-teams-say-design-handoff-is-broken-heres-how-to-fix-it-with-figma-to-azure-5h7)).
3. **Figma Dev Mode helps but only if designers treat file prep as part of the build, not an afterthought**: it centralizes information engineers previously had to chase down from designers directly, reducing but not eliminating friction, contingent on designer discipline in defining states/tokens ahead of handoff ([Figma developer handoff guide](https://www.figma.com/best-practices/guide-to-developer-handoff/), [DEV Community](https://dev.to/hunterstein/91-of-teams-say-design-handoff-is-broken-heres-how-to-fix-it-with-figma-to-azure-5h7)).
- Researcher → PM handoff friction specifically: not directly sourced this pass beyond the broader finding that PMs are increasingly running their own research (see Practice variation section), which itself signals a handoff being replaced rather than merely improved [partially sourced, see below].

## Gates and decision criteria

- **Accessibility gate**: best-practice guidance states accessibility checklists should be included in design review cycles and that color contrast should be validated *before* sign-off — i.e., accessibility is framed as a pre-sign-off checklist item within design review, not a separate downstream QA-only gate ([Bird Eats Bug](https://birdeatsbug.com/blog/wcag-for-designers)).
- **Designer-engineer-QA shared responsibility for accessibility**: guidance explicitly rejects treating WCAG as solely a developer responsibility — designers are expected to collaborate with developers and QA so design intent (contrast, typography, layout, alt text, focus order, ARIA roles) survives into accessible code; most real-world products target WCAG Level AA as the practical bar ([BrowserStack](https://www.browserstack.com/guide/wcag-for-designers), [UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/a-designers-guide-to-wcag-accessibility-15d96b3ff09f)).
- Formal "definition of done" for design work, explicit design-critique sign-off authority, and research-readiness gates were **not directly sourced** with primary evidence in this pass beyond the accessibility-in-design-review finding above [unsourced].

## Practice variation and open debates

- **Embedded vs. centralized design teams**: centralized teams ease knowledge-sharing/standards and broaden designer skill exposure and career growth, but risk designers being isolated from ground-level product context, discovering constraints late, and slow iteration; embedded teams reduce context-switching and build cross-functional trust/velocity, but risk siloing and can limit a designer's career growth due to fixed use cases. The right model reportedly depends on org size, product complexity, and maturity — startups lean embedded for speed, larger enterprises lean centralized for consistency ([UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/3-design-team-structures-centralized-embedded-and-the-elevator-team-f2970008ed41), [Superside](https://www.superside.com/blog/design-team)).
- **UX research is being cut and absorbed into PM roles (2024–2026 shift)**: Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, and Google reportedly cut user-research roles harder than most other functions during layoff waves (~152,922 tech jobs cut in 2024, ~122,549 in 2025 industry-wide); UX research job postings fell below 1,000 in early 2025; a cited 2024 industry report found 47% of companies that laid off UX staff reassigned design responsibilities to product teams; maturing AI research tooling is enabling PMs and CS managers to run their own studies with AI handling moderation/transcription/synthesis, replacing the dedicated-researcher handoff rather than just streamlining it ([Perspective AI 2026 report](https://getperspective.ai/blog/the-2026-state-of-customer-research-hiring-why-teams-cut-researchers-and-bought-ai), [Medium: The Disappearing UX Director](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/the-disappearing-ux-director-are-uxers-becoming-product-managers-554b65968da0), [AcademyUX Q1 2025 job market analysis](https://blog.academyux.com/ux-job-market-analysis-q1-2025/)).
- **Designer-to-engineer ratios vary widely and lack consensus**: industry-average designer:developer ratio cited as 1:10 to 1:20, though top tech companies run tighter at 1:5 to 1:8; full-team PM:UX:Engineering ratios commonly cited in the 1:1:5 to 1:1:10 range; when researchers are included, a 2020 survey found the most typical researcher:designer:developer ratio was 1:5:50; real-world variation spans from 1:1:1 up to roughly 1:1:50, undercutting any single "correct" benchmark ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-developer-ratio/), [The Org](https://theorg.com/iterate/ideal-ratio-for-pm-ux-and-engineering), [UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/designer-to-developer-ratio/)).
- **The Nielsen "5 users" claim is disputed within the field itself**: originally framed as a general rule for usability testing, it is now understood (including by NN/g's own later writing) to apply specifically to qualitative testing with a uniform user group, requiring more users for quantitative testing or distinct user segments; academic critics dispute even the qualitative claim, and practitioners warn it has been over-applied as a blanket justification for under-testing before launch ([NN/g "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/), [ResearchGate: "Why and when five test users aren't enough"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200553185_Why_and_when_five_test_users_aren't_enough), [PixelTree Media](https://pixeltreemedia.co.uk/usability-testing-with-5-users-is-it-enough/)).
- **Design systems' effect on centralization**: not conclusively sourced this pass whether design systems reduce or centralize design work overall — the Design Systems Engineer role material implies systems concentrate reusable-component work into a dedicated team/role (a centralizing effect) while theoretically freeing product designers from rebuilding basics, but no source directly debated the net effect on total design labor [partially sourced, interpretation only — see DevOpsSchool].
- Methodologies not directly re-sourced with dedicated searches this pass beyond design sprints and HEART: discount usability, contextual inquiry, diary studies, card sorting, tree testing, Kano-style expectation research — searched jointly with design sprints but the search engine returned only design-sprint-specific results, so these remain **[unsourced]** in this report and would need separate targeted searches.
- **Google's HEART framework**: created in 2010 by Kerry Rodden, Hilary Hutchinson, and Xin Fu on Google's research team, first published at ACM CHI; stands for Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task Success; pairs with a Goals-Signals-Metrics (GSM) process; cited as the most-referenced UX measurement framework in product management, with Atlassian, Spotify, and GoDaddy referenced as adopters in secondary sources ([Statsig](https://www.statsig.com/perspectives/heart-framework-measuring-ux), [IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/google-s-heart-framework-for-measuring-ux)).
- **Design sprints**: the 5-day GV design sprint format (define, sketch, decide, prototype, test with customers) was created by Jake Knapp starting at Google in 2010 (Chrome, Search, Google X teams), brought to GV in 2012, and refined with Braden Kowitz, Michael Margolis, John Zeratsky, and Daniel Burka before being published in the 2016 book *Sprint* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_sprint), [GV](https://www.gv.com/sprint/), [jakeknapp.com](https://jakeknapp.com/sprint)).
