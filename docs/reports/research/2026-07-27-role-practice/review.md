# The Practice of Independent Review and Audit

This report grounds the design of a `review` agent role (states idle/scoped/auditing/reported, verdicts Present/Surface/Absent/Incorrect, working from "the change and the specification" without builder intent) in how independent review and audit are actually practiced by humans: code review, design review, security review, and compliance audit, at the granularity of individual work products and judgment calls.

## What the work actually is

**Modern lightweight code review.** Google's internal code review guide is the most detailed public description of a large-scale review practice. Its explicit standard is *not* "is this code perfect" but "does this change improve the overall code health of the system" — reviewers are told not to require authors to polish every point before approving, and a change that clearly improves maintainability/readability/understandability "shouldn't be delayed for days or weeks because it isn't perfect" (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html). The reviewer's job is scoped to design, functionality, complexity, tests, naming, comments, style, and documentation (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/). Empirically, at Google most changes are small — median change size is around 24 lines, 90% of reviews touch fewer than 10 files — and turnaround is fast: under 1 hour median first feedback for changes under 100 lines, versus 24+ hours for changes over 1,000 lines, with a stated finding that review quality drops above ~200 lines (https://graphite.com/blog/how-google-does-code-review, https://www.michaelagreiler.com/code-reviews-at-google/). Google's own guidance is to keep changes small for exactly this reason (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html).

Academically, Bacchelli and Bird's study of Microsoft engineers ("Expectations, Outcomes, and Challenges of Modern Code Review", ICSE 2013) found that modern, lightweight review is in practice *less* about defect-hunting than expected: reviewers surveyed and interviewed reported that review's realized value is more about knowledge transfer, team awareness, and finding alternative solutions than about catching bugs, and that review today is far less formal than the 1970s–80s inspection model it descended from (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/ICSE202013-codereview.pdf, https://sback.it/publications/icse2013.pdf). This is a load-bearing finding for a `review` agent role: it means the discipline of "does this match the spec" is closer to the *older*, more formal inspection tradition than to what most humans mean by "code review" today — the role as specified (verdict against a spec, not holistic quality judgment) is deliberately reaching back past ordinary PR review toward inspection-style rigor.

**Fagan inspection and its process shape.** Michael Fagan's original 1976 IBM Systems Journal paper, "Design and Code Inspections to Reduce Errors in Program Development" (IBM Systems Journal 15:182–211), reported design/code inspection able to catch roughly 82% of defects found over a product's life during the inspection stage itself, with follow-on industry reports of 80–90% detection rates (https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1131845, search-derived summary of Fagan 1976; SEI teaching materials at https://www.sei.cmu.edu/documents/1561/1993_011_001_16127.pdf). The process has a fixed shape that later "lightweight" review abandoned: **planning** (select the work product, assign roles, schedule), an **overview meeting**, individual **preparation** (each inspector reviews the artifact alone first), an **inspection meeting** (walkthrough with defects logged, not fixed live), **rework**, and **follow-up** to verify fixes (https://www.professionalqa.com/fagan-inspection, https://www.professionalqa.com/inspection-roles-participants-and-process). The defined roles are **moderator** (schedules, runs the meeting, reports results, chases follow-up), **author** (created the artifact, present to answer questions but not to defend it), **reader** (paraphrases the work product line by line so the team engages with what's actually there, not what the author says is there), **recorder** (logs defects), and **inspectors** (every participant, whose job is to find errors) (same sources). Two design features are directly relevant to an adversarial reviewer role: (1) the reader — not the author — narrates the artifact, which is the same "no builder intent" isolation the `review` role already encodes; (2) there is an explicit **follow-up** step to verify rework, which the idle/scoped/auditing/reported state list does not currently have (see Candidates section).

**Threat modelling as a review activity** and **checklist-based inspection** are both operationalized in the security research already available to this agent (STRIDE, DREAD skills) and in the prior QA/security reports — not re-derived here, but the empirical severity-classification material below (Chromium, Microsoft bug bar) is the concrete artifact-shape evidence for how a security "review" turns into a graded verdict.

**Audit sampling (financial/compliance audit).** Financial auditors do not examine 100% of transactions; AICPA's Audit Sampling framework (aligned to AU-C 530) formalizes **statistical sampling** — where sample size and result evaluation are governed by probability theory so sampling risk can be quantified — versus **non-statistical sampling**, and ties sample size to three explicit inputs: desired confidence level, expected deviation rate, and tolerable deviation rate (https://legalclarity.org/aicpa-audit-guide-key-concepts-in-audit-sampling/, https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/auditing/aicpa/audit-accounting-guides-audit-risk-alerts/audit-sampling). This is the closest human-practice analogue to a `review` agent deciding how much of a large diff it can actually audit at full rigor versus sample — audit practice treats "what fraction of the population must I examine to support a verdict at a stated confidence" as a first-class, explicitly quantified decision, not an implicit one.

**Reported pace figures, gathered in one place:**
- Cisco/SmartBear study (SmartBear, ~2500 reviews / 3.2M LOC, 10 months): optimal review size 100–300 LOC, defect detection best under ~300 LOC/hour, reviewers faster than 450 LOC/hour showed below-average defect density in 87% of cases, optimal session length 30–60 minutes with defect detection rates plummeting past ~90 minutes, average defect density ~32 per kLOC, 61% of reviews in the sample found *no* defects at all, and reviews with author-supplied annotations/context showed markedly lower (often zero) defect counts (https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf, summarized secondhand via https://mikeconley.ca/blog/2009/09/14/smart-bear-cisco-and-the-largest-study-on-code-review-ever/).
- Fagan inspection: ~82% defect detection reported in the original IBM data, with later industry reports of 80–90% (sources above).
- Google: median ~24 LOC per change, 90% under 10 files, sub-1-hour median first feedback for small changes, 24+ hour median for 1000+ line changes (https://graphite.com/blog/how-google-does-code-review).

## Artifacts and their shapes

This is the section most directly reusable for a review-role's state-file and skill outputs.

**CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) v3.1 vector and fields** — the canonical severity-scoring artifact for security findings (https://www.first.org/cvss/v3.1/specification-document, https://www.first.org/cvss/v3-1/cvss-v31-specification_r1.pdf):
- A vector string is mandatory-complete on all **Base** metrics; **Temporal** and **Environmental** metrics are optional and default to "Not Defined (X)" if omitted.
- Format: `CVSS:3.1/` followed by slash-delimited `MetricAbbrev:Value` pairs; no metric may repeat; readers must tolerate any ordering even though a canonical order is recommended.
- Base metric groups (from the specification): Attack Vector, Attack Complexity, Privileges Required, User Interaction, Scope, and the Impact triad (Confidentiality/Integrity/Availability). A finding record that wants CVSS-shaped severity needs, at minimum, fields for each of these plus the resulting Base Score and severity band (None/Low/Medium/High/Critical per FIRST's rating scale).

**DREAD** — Damage potential, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users, Discoverability, each scored (originally 1–10, later often High/Medium/Low) and combined/averaged into an overall rating (https://www.eccouncil.org/cybersecurity-exchange/threat-intelligence/dread-threat-modeling-intro/, https://threat-modeling.com/dread-threat-modeling/). **Disagreement to record explicitly:** Microsoft *abandoned* DREAD for its own SDL and no longer recommends it, citing its subjectivity and the lack of concrete guidance for scoring each factor consistently across reviewers (https://blog.stackaware.com/p/a-somewhat-dreadful-vulnerability, https://blog.stackaware.com/p/hurdling-the-bug-bar). It still sees use elsewhere (small businesses, some large enterprises) but is not the field's converged answer (https://www.eccouncil.org/cybersecurity-exchange/threat-intelligence/dread-threat-modeling-intro/).

**Microsoft SDL "Bug Bar"** — the structured replacement for DREAD: a fixed lookup table (not a computed average) with four severity levels — **Critical / Important / Moderate / Low** — each defined by concrete, enumerated technical criteria per vulnerability class (elevation of privilege, information disclosure, tampering, spoofing, denial of service, security-feature bypass), separately tabulated for **server** and **client** targets (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security/engineering/Security-Bug-Bar-sample). Key structural features worth reusing in a rulebook:
- Severity is looked up from *characteristics of the vulnerability* (attacker authentication state: anonymous vs authenticated; scope: default/common vs special-configuration scenario; persistence: temporary vs permanent), not computed from a weighted-average subjective score — this is explicitly the anti-DREAD design choice.
- A **DoS matrix** cross-tabulates (authenticated/anonymous) × (default/scenario) × (temporary/temporary-with-amplification/permanent) → rating, i.e., severity bands are a deterministic table lookup over a small number of orthogonal, objectively-observable axes, not a holistic judgment call.
- A **NEAT** qualifier (Necessary / Explained / Actionable / Tested) is applied specifically to warning/prompt UX to decide whether "the user was warned" should reduce severity — a mitigating-factor framework, not just a raw severity.

**Chromium security severity guidelines** — five bands, **Critical (S0) / High (S1) / Medium (S2) / Low (S3) / Unknown (S4)**, each defined by concrete capability (arbitrary code/resource access with full privilege = Critical; cross-origin data read or code exec in another origin's context = High; limited info read/modify or harmless-alone-but-combinable = Medium; higher-severity-in-theory but with extreme mitigating/limited-scope factors = Low), each tied to a disclosure/patch SLA (Critical: 60-day public disclosure norm, 7 days if actively exploited; High: patch to all users within 60 days) (https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/security/severity-guidelines.md, https://www.chromium.org/developers/severity-guidelines/). The pattern to note: severity classification is bundled with a **response-time commitment**, not scored in isolation.

**OWASP finding/report structure** (Web Security Testing Guide reporting chapter and community vulnerability template) — a finding record's canonical field list (https://owasp.org/www-project-web-security-testing-guide/latest/5-Reporting/01-Reporting_Structure, https://owasp.org/www-community/vulnerabilities/Vulnerability_template):
- Title
- Severity/Risk rating (Informational / Low / Medium / High / Critical, detailed in an appendix), with CVSS score where applicable
- Likelihood/exploitability, itself a function of: ease of exploitation, existence of working exploit code, level of access required, attacker motivation
- Affected asset(s)
- Description of the vulnerability
- Evidence / proof of concept (request/response payloads, screenshots, exploit chain — with sensitive data masked)
- Impact
- CWE classification / mapping
- Remediation guidance

This is directly the shape a `review` role's per-requirement finding record should generalize: **claim/requirement id → verdict → severity/risk → evidence (pointer into the diff, not a claim) → impact/rationale → (for compliance) reference to the specific spec clause**.

**Fagan inspection's artifact**: a **defect list**, produced and owned by the recorder during the inspection meeting, distinct from the author's rework — i.e., the finding record is authored by someone *other than* the artifact's creator, matching the "no builder intent" isolation already in the `review` role design.

**Audit trail / sign-off**: financial audit practice frames sampling itself as an auditable decision — sample size derivation (confidence level, expected deviation rate, tolerable deviation rate) is retained as part of the audit record, not just the sampled conclusion (https://legalclarity.org/aicpa-audit-guide-key-concepts-in-audit-sampling/). For a review role, this suggests the state file should retain not just verdicts but *what fraction/which parts of the change were actually inspected at what rigor*, so a downstream consumer can tell "audited in full" from "sampled."

## Decision criteria and gates

**Blocking vs. nit**, per Google's reviewer standard: block if the change "definitely worsens the overall code health of the system" or introduces something the reviewer has an actual system-level objection to; everything else that's polish-only should be prefixed "Nit:" and explicitly framed as something the author may ignore without blocking approval (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html). Google also explicitly warns against letting review stall indefinitely over unresolved disagreement.

**Severity bands** — three converging shapes across sources, useful as alternatives to weigh: CVSS's continuous 0–10 score computed from Base (+Temporal +Environmental) metrics with mapped qualitative bands (None/Low/Medium/High/Critical) (https://www.first.org/cvss/v3.1/specification-document); Microsoft's bug-bar 4-level table lookup (Critical/Important/Moderate/Low) driven by enumerated objective criteria rather than a computed score (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security/engineering/Security-Bug-Bar-sample); Chromium's 5-level scheme (Critical/High/Medium/Low/Unknown) tied to disclosure SLAs (https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/security/severity-guidelines.md). **The disagreement worth recording explicitly**: DREAD's weighted-average-of-five-subjective-factors approach was tried and *abandoned* by its own originating organization for being too subjective and insufficiently guided, in favor of Microsoft's table-lookup bug bar (https://blog.stackaware.com/p/a-somewhat-dreadful-vulnerability, https://blog.stackaware.com/p/hurdling-the-bug-bar). The lesson for rulebook design: prefer deterministic classification from observable, enumerable characteristics of the finding (as bug bar and CWE/CVSS do) over an averaged subjective multi-factor score (as DREAD does).

**Empirical detection-rate numbers, gathered for gate-threshold justification:**
- Fagan-style formal inspection: ~80–90% defect detection reported in industry follow-ups to the original 82% IBM figure (sources above) — this is the ceiling case for a highly formal process.
- Ad hoc/lightweight modern code review, per the Cisco/SmartBear study: 70–90% defect discovery *achievable* but only within the 200–400 LOC / 30–90 minute window; 61% of actual reviews in the study sample found zero defects, suggesting the achievable rate is frequently not realized in practice (https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf).
- **Size threshold**: 200–400 LOC is the repeatedly cited ceiling before defect-detection effectiveness degrades (SmartBear/Cisco); Google's independent data likewise shows review quality dropping above ~200 lines and turnaround time growing sharply past that size (https://graphite.com/blog/how-google-does-code-review).
- **Time threshold**: 30–60 minutes optimal, defect detection "plummets" past ~90 minutes (SmartBear/Cisco, same source) — directly informs a `review` agent's own session-scoping (how much of a diff to audit per pass before quality degrades, even though the agent doesn't literally get "tired," this bounds how much unrelated context should be crammed into one auditing pass without losing precision).
- **Rate threshold**: below ~300–400 LOC/hour correlates with above-average defect detection; above ~450 LOC/hour, defect density found is below average in 87% of sampled reviews (same source) — i.e., speed itself is evidence of shallow review, not just a side effect of it.

**Block vs. advise, generalized**: Google's model blocks only on system-health regression, not on imperfection; security bug bars fold in explicit disclosure/patch SLAs proportional to severity (i.e., higher severity doesn't just get a label, it gets a *harder gate* — shorter time-to-fix, mandatory before ship for Critical/High); OWASP-style pentest findings are usually advisory to the client with a remediation deadline rather than an automatic hard stop, except where a report is explicitly gating a release/compliance sign-off.

## Failure modes

**Rubber-stamp / LGTM culture.** Widely documented in industry writing (not peer-reviewed, and marked as such): approvals given as a social lubricant rather than a genuine assessment, review comments reduced to "LGTM" without evidence the reviewer engaged with the diff, and cited real consequences (a shipped crash and a shipped security vulnerability despite multiple "LGTM" approvals) (https://medium.com/@baristaGeek/just-lgtm-on-pull-request-comments-youre-failing-as-a-dev-e389ee87f598, https://grenovales.medium.com/the-hidden-risks-of-looks-good-to-me-9fb873240927). Countermeasure commonly proposed: reserve LGTM/approval for *after* automated checks, ownership rules, and any required risk review have already passed — i.e., approval should never be the first or only signal (same sources). `[unsourced]` The oft-cited "65% of PRs get rubber-stamped" figure came from a search-engine synthesis without a traceable primary study and should be treated as anecdotal, not empirical.

**Reviewer/decision fatigue.** An empirical study at WirelessCar (arXiv, cited via search) identified reviewer fatigue, context switching, and inconsistent review depth as persistent real problems in code review practice, and found developers valued AI-generated summaries specifically to counteract this in large/unfamiliar diffs (https://arxiv.org/html/2505.16339v1). Confirmation bias and decision fatigue are separately documented as triggered by review volume and unfamiliar-topic assignment (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01407). This directly supports the SmartBear/Cisco finding above that speed and volume correlate with degraded detection, and supports scoping an automated reviewer's per-pass workload rather than assuming an agent is immune to the "more material, shallower pass" failure mode simply because it isn't biologically tired.

**Nitpicking as substitute for substance.** Google's guide structurally addresses this by requiring nits to be explicitly labeled "Nit:" and by making clear they must not block approval — the underlying failure mode being that reviewers who lack confidence to assess substance retreat to style/naming commentary they *can* confidently assess (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html).

**Findings with no reproduction / evidence.** OWASP's report template structurally forces this: every finding requires Evidence/Proof-of-Concept as a named field, not optional prose (https://owasp.org/www-community/vulnerabilities/Vulnerability_template). A finding lacking a pointer into the actual artifact is, by this standard, incomplete regardless of how confidently it's asserted.

**Severity inflation.** `[unsourced]` — no primary empirical study of severity-inflation rates was found in this pass; the CVSS and bug-bar literature's insistence on *criteria-driven* (rather than gut-feel) severity assignment is itself an implicit countermeasure (deterministic table lookup removes the discretion that inflation exploits), but no direct study quantifying inflation rates or its countermeasure's effectiveness was located and fetched.

**Audit theatre.** `[unsourced]` — this report did not locate and fetch a primary empirical source specifically on "audit theatre" / compliance-audit-as-performance as a named, studied phenomenon; the AICPA sampling-rigor material (explicit confidence levels, tolerable deviation rates, retained sampling-derivation records) is the closest sourced countermeasure found — it makes the *rigor itself*, not just the conclusion, auditable after the fact, which is the structural antidote to an audit that only produces a conclusion with no traceable basis.

## Tooling and automation

**SAST false-positive rates are large and vary hugely by tool.** Industry benchmarking reports legacy SAST tools running 68–78% average false-positive rates on common languages, with some production deployments reporting false positives as high as 95%; against this, purpose-tuned tools on the OWASP Benchmark project claim much lower rates (one vendor reporting 2.1%, another 16.7% FPR at 100% true-positive rate, SonarQube as low as 1% in specific benchmark configurations) (https://www.mend.io/blog/benchmark-of-false-positives/, https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250305353933/en/Cycode-SAST-Leaves-Competitors-Behind-with-94-Fewer-False-Positives-in-OWASP-Benchmark, https://xygeni.io/blog/top-sast-tools/). Reported downstream cost: security teams reportedly spend 15–30 minutes triaging *each* false positive versus 4+ hours to actually fix a real vulnerability — meaning a high-FPR tool can consume more reviewer time than it saves (https://www.mend.io/blog/benchmark-of-false-positives/). The **OWASP Benchmark Project** itself is the standard mechanism the field uses to measure SAST accuracy: a deliberately-seeded vulnerable sample application used to compute true/false positive rates comparably across tools (referenced across the above sources).

**Division of labor implied**: automated SAST/linters are well suited to pattern-matchable, syntactically-detectable classes (known-bad API usage, missing sanitization patterns, style/naming) but generate enough noise that a human/agent triage step is mandatory before a finding is trusted — the tool proposes, a reviewer (human or the `review` agent) disposes. This matches Fagan inspection's own layering: automatable checks are not a substitute for the inspection meeting's human judgment, only a pre-filter for it.

## Candidates for rulebook encoding

*(Everything in this section is this report's own synthesis, not a sourced claim.)*

**(a) Is the four-verdict taxonomy (Present/Surface/Absent/Incorrect) sound?** Partially, with one clear gap. Every sourced finding taxonomy above (CVSS, bug bar, CWE/OWASP, Chromium) is built to classify **the severity/nature of a confirmed deviation**, not to classify **whether a deviation exists at all** — that binary/quaternary existence-classification step is closer to what Fagan inspection's "defect list" does (log an observed discrepancy against a spec, yes/no) than to any severity scale. Present/Surface/Absent/Incorrect is doing a different, and arguably prior, job: it's an *existence-and-fidelity* classification, not a *severity* classification. That's coherent and is not undermined by anything sourced here. The gap: none of the four verdicts captures **"cannot be determined from the given evidence"** — real audit practice (both Fagan inspection's rework/follow-up loop and AICPA's tolerable-deviation-rate framing) explicitly allows for "insufficient evidence to conclude," which is different from Absent (verifiably not there) or Incorrect (verifiably wrong). Recommend adding a fifth verdict, **Unverifiable** (already used by the sibling `implementation-audit` skill, per its own description) or folding "insufficient evidence" as a documented sub-case of one existing verdict — but as currently specified, a requirement that the reviewer genuinely cannot check (e.g., a runtime/operational requirement unobservable from a static diff) has no honest place to go among the four.

**(b) What must a finding record contain to be actionable?** Drawing directly on the OWASP field list and the CVSS/bug-bar precedent: at minimum — (1) a stable identifier for the specific spec requirement/claim being checked; (2) the verdict; (3) an **evidence pointer** into the actual change (file/line/diff hunk), not a paraphrase — mirroring OWASP's mandatory Evidence/PoC field and Fagan's reader-narrates-the-artifact discipline; (4) a one-line rationale connecting evidence to verdict; (5) for Incorrect verdicts specifically, what the spec required vs. what was actually built (the CWE/CVSS pattern of describing the *class* of deviation, not just "wrong"); (6) severity/impact *only if* the role's scope is extended beyond pure fidelity-checking into risk-weighting — the current role description says it produces per-requirement verdicts, not holistic judgment, so severity may be deliberately out of scope, but if any downstream gate needs to prioritize a list of Incorrect/Absent findings, at least a coarse severity field (à la Chromium's four/five bands, not DREAD's averaged score — per the sourced disagreement above) would be needed to make the report actionable rather than just complete.

**(c) Are idle/scoped/auditing/reported the right states?** Missing at least one state relative to real practice on two independent lines of evidence. Fagan inspection has an explicit **follow-up** phase after the inspection meeting to verify that rework actually addressed logged defects — the inspection is not considered closed until follow-up confirms it. AICPA audit practice similarly treats the audit's sampling/conclusion as something that gets checked, not just asserted. The current four states end at `reported`, with no state representing "the builder responded/fixed something and the review needs to re-check." Two ways to close this gap: either (i) treat re-audits as a fresh idle→scoped→auditing→reported cycle scoped only to the delta (defensible, keeps the state machine simple, matches "independent auditor" framing — the reviewer doesn't need a special state to look at a diff again), or (ii) add an explicit **verification/re-check** state distinct from a fresh `auditing` pass, which would let the state file distinguish "first audit of this requirement" from "confirming a fix," and would let a rulebook require narrower, faster re-checks (arguably reusing the SmartBear/Cisco pacing data — a re-check of one fixed requirement should be a much smaller-scope, faster pass than the original full audit). Given the role is explicitly not supposed to fix things or track builder intent, (i) is probably the cleaner fit — but the rulebook should make explicit that a "re-check" is a legitimate, distinct trigger into `scoped`, scoped only to the previously-flagged requirements, rather than assuming every re-entry into the state machine re-audits everything from scratch. A dedicated **triage** state between `scoped` and `auditing` was considered against the audit-sampling evidence (deciding sample size/rigor before auditing) but is likely unnecessary as a separate *state* — it's better encoded as a required field/decision recorded upon entering `auditing` (what fraction of the change will be audited at full rigor vs. sampled, per the AICPA precedent of recording the sampling derivation) rather than as its own state transition.
