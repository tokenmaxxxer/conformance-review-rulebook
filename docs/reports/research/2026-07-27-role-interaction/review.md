# When and How an Independent Reviewer Involves a Human

This report is scoped to the human-in-the-loop transitions of a `review` agent role: not what the role produces (see the companion report on finding records, severity taxonomies, and review pace, `docs/reports/research/2026-07-27-role-practice/review.md`), but the recurring moments where an independent reviewer/auditor cannot proceed alone and must stop to involve another human — carrying what, to whom, returning with what. The role reports findings and does not fix, so every interaction below is about agreeing scope, contesting a finding, or handing over — never about remediation itself.

## Moments that call for a human

**1. Agreeing scope and what is out of bounds, before starting.** Internal audit practice fixes objectives, scope, and boundaries with the auditee before fieldwork begins, typically at or before the entrance conference — Binghamton University's internal audit process page describes the entrance conference as where "the purpose, scope, and process of the audit" are discussed with the unit head (https://www.binghamton.edu/offices/internal-audit/audit-process.html). Penetration-test engagements formalize this even more tightly as a pre-engagement contract: DeepStrike's guidance on pen-test contracts stresses that scope, rules of engagement, and out-of-bounds systems must be defined and signed before testing starts (https://deepstrike.io/blog/penetration-testing-contract). What the reviewer brings: a proposed objective/scope statement. Who they bring it to: the unit head / engagement sponsor (audit) or the client's technical and legal point of contact (pentest). What they leave with: an agreed, often written, scope boundary that the rest of the engagement is measured against.

**2. The entrance/kickoff meeting.** A distinct, named event in both traditions. IIA-aligned practice: "Internal audit entrance conferences are critical in discussing the scope and objectives of departments being audited" (https://www.richardchambers.com/five-things-not-to-say-during-an-internal-audit-entrance-conference/, mirrored at https://iaonline.theiia.org/five-things-not-to-say-during-an-internal-audit-entrance-conference). GAO's Yellow Book practice is similar at a government-to-government scale: GAO holds entrance conferences with the responsible officials before starting audit work (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-185r tracks whether these conferences with DOD are even being held on time, underscoring how load-bearing the step is treated to be). What's brought: the audit plan/objectives. Who to: the auditee's management. What's left with: acknowledgment of scope, a point of contact, and an agreed timeline for fieldwork.

**3. Requesting evidence or access the reviewer lacks.** Not separately sourced as its own named ceremony in this pass, but implicit in every audit-sampling and pentest-scoping source: the reviewer's evidentiary access (documents, environments, log retention, sampling population) is negotiated with the auditee's system owner as it comes up during fieldwork, not just at kickoff. `[unsourced]` — no primary source in this pass documents a formal, separately-named "evidence request" ceremony distinct from ordinary fieldwork correspondence; treat this as a real but informally-documented moment.

**4. Confirming a suspected finding with the owner before publishing it.** Penetration-test practice: reputable providers walk the client through the discovery steps and reproduction path so that findings are validated with the owner's technical staff before the final report locks — SoftwareSecured's own account of dispute-handling notes that "penetration testing reports include the exact steps the testers used and how you can reproduce the same results," specifically so the client can verify a finding before/at reporting time rather than being surprised by it (https://www.softwaresecured.com/post/what-to-do-when-you-dont-agree-with-your-penetration-test-report). In IIA-aligned internal audit, this is the exit-conference draft-review step (see below) — the draft, containing tentative findings, is walked through with management before the report is final.

**5. The disputed-finding process — the owner disagrees, who adjudicates.** This is the moment where the three traditions genuinely disagree on procedure and authority, and that disagreement is itself worth recording (see below):
   - **Pentest**: client schedules a meeting with the testing team; testers walk through their discovery process again; if still unresolved, the client is advised to bring in an independent third-party security consultant to review the report, tester notes, and reproducibility evidence (https://www.softwaresecured.com/post/what-to-do-when-you-dont-agree-with-your-penetration-test-report).
   - **Code review (Google)**: "the first step should always be for the developer and reviewer to try to come to consensus"; if that fails, a face-to-face or video conversation is recommended (with the outcome then recorded back as a CL comment for posterity); if that still fails, escalation runs to "a broader team discussion, having a Technical Lead weigh in, asking for a decision from a maintainer of the code, or asking an Eng Manager to help out" (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/pushback.html, https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html). Google's own written hierarchy for resolving the *substance* of a disagreement: technical facts beat opinion, the style guide is authoritative on style, engineering principles (not taste) govern design, and if the author can show multiple approaches are equally valid the reviewer must yield to author preference.
   - **IS audit (ISACA)**: "if the auditee disagrees with the impact of a finding, it is important for an IS auditor to elaborate and clarify the risks and exposures, as the auditee may not fully appreciate the magnitude of the exposure" — i.e., the auditor's first move is persuasion, not escalation (https://www.briefmenow.org/isaca/during-an-exit-interview-in-cases-where-there-is-disagreement-regarding-the-impact-of-a-finding-an-is-auditor-should/). If disagreement persists, "disagreements with management over audit results may escalate to senior leadership or the audit committee. Ultimately, the audit committee may have to serve as the final authority in resolving disputes over audit findings" (search-derived synthesis grounded in ISACA guidance, corroborated by https://www.richardchambers.com/when-the-audit-resolution-process-turns-into-a-food-fight/ on how these disputes escalate in practice).
   - **Financial/compliance audit**: management can partially agree — "accepts the condition but disputes the cause or effect assessment," commonly where management asserts a compensating control mitigates the risk; the auditor may retain the finding but adjust the risk rating rather than drop it (https://auditingauthority.com/audit-findings-and-management-response/).

   The disagreement worth recording explicitly: code review resolves peer-to-peer with escalation to a technical authority (Tech Lead/maintainer/manager) over a *technical* question; audit resolves via persuasion first, then to a *governance* authority (audit committee/board) over a *risk-acceptance* question — the adjudicator in audit is not a more-senior technical expert but a governance body with formal sign-off power. The reviewer never unilaterally overrules the disagreement into silence in either tradition — it either gets resolved by consensus, sent to a named third party, or published with the disagreement stated (see Section 3).

**6. Severity disagreement specifically.** Distinguished from disputing the finding's existence: in compliance audit, the auditor may keep a finding but re-rate its risk level when management presents a compensating control (https://auditingauthority.com/audit-findings-and-management-response/) — the finding survives, only severity is renegotiated. `[unsourced]` — no source in this pass describes a severity-specific escalation path distinct from the general disputed-finding path in code review or pentest; treat severity disagreement as following the same escalation ladder as any other disputed finding in those traditions.

**7. The exit meeting and how findings are handed over.** IIA-aligned practice: "At the exit conference, the report draft is reviewed so that all of the parties understand the nature of the recommendations and agree upon the possible solutions... The Internal Auditor presents a summary of observations and gathers cause statements from management" (https://www.binghamton.edu/offices/internal-audit/audit-process.html). GAO/DOD protocol requires participants to review the statement of facts (SOF) and any other information provided *before* the exit conference so they can give "useful and fully developed feedback" at the meeting itself — i.e., the document is pre-circulated, the meeting is for reconciling reactions to it, not first disclosure (per DOD Instruction 7650.02, https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/765002p.pdf). What's brought: the draft report/findings. Who to: the auditee's management (and, for higher-severity items, the audit committee). What's left with: an agreed (or agreed-to-disagree) findings set that becomes the near-final report, plus assigned owners and target dates for corrective action.

**8. Who accepts residual risk when a finding will not be fixed.** IIA's *Internal Auditor* magazine on risk acceptance: "Risk acceptance is used when other risk response options are unavailable or not optimal." Approval authority is tiered by severity — moderate risks are approved by the business-unit leader, high risks require CEO approval. Internal audit's own role here is explicitly *not* to accept or reject the risk itself but to act as "thought partner and validator": it can concur with management's acceptance decision or "articulate their reasoning" if it disagrees, and continues monitoring the accepted risk over time in case conditions change (https://internalauditor.theiia.org/en/articles/2022/february/risk-acceptance/). Where the accepted risk exceeds the enterprise's stated risk appetite, ISACA guidance says this must be escalated further, to the audit committee and/or board (search-derived synthesis of ISACA guidance, cross-referenced above). This is the clearest example in all the sourced material of the reviewer *not* being the adjudicator of its own findings — the reviewer validates and monitors; a risk owner outside the review role accepts.

## The shape of the exchange

- **Entrance conference**: a scheduled, synchronous meeting, held before fieldwork starts, between the reviewer and the auditee's management/unit head. Content: audit purpose, scope, objectives, methodology, expected timeframe; opportunity for the auditee to ask questions (https://www.binghamton.edu/offices/internal-audit/audit-process.html, DOD Instruction 7650.02 https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/765002p.pdf). Recorded as meeting minutes/an engagement memo, not embedded finding-by-finding in the report body.
- **Exit conference**: a scheduled, synchronous meeting at (or near) the end of fieldwork. Content is the *draft* report — the reviewer walks through observations, gathers management's cause statements and reactions (https://www.binghamton.edu/offices/internal-audit/audit-process.html). GAO/DOD practice requires the statement-of-facts document to be reviewed by participants *before* the meeting so the meeting itself is spent on substantive feedback rather than first-read reactions (https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/765002p.pdf).
- **Management response, written by the auditee**: a formal, written artifact attached to each finding (not just to the report as a whole) in the audit convention. Concretely: "A written response to the draft report is required within 3 weeks of receiving the draft report. Management's responses must include the unit's plan for corrective action, the name and title of the person responsible for implementing the corrective action and the date by which the action will be implemented" (search-derived synthesis of internal-audit-office practice, e.g. patterns documented across university/agency internal audit offices found via search, exemplified at https://www.binghamton.edu/offices/internal-audit/audit-process.html). Financial/compliance audit generalizes this into an explicit response *type* per finding — full agreement with a corrective action plan, or partial agreement (accepts the finding, disputes cause/effect, cites a compensating control) — recorded alongside the finding rather than as a separate document (https://auditingauthority.com/audit-findings-and-management-response/).
- **PR comment threads (code review)**: asynchronous, inline, per-line or per-hunk, no fixed length; escalation to synchronous contact (face-to-face or video call) is a documented *exception* path used "when coming to consensus becomes especially difficult," with the explicit instruction to write the outcome back into the CL as a comment so the async record stays complete (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/pushback.html). This is the async-first / sync-as-escalation pattern, inverse to audit's meeting-first convention.
- **Findings walkthrough (pentest)**: not a fixed-length ceremony in the sourced material, but explicitly reproducible-by-design — "reports include the exact steps the testers used and how you can reproduce the same results," so that a walkthrough, when needed, is a re-execution of the same evidence rather than a fresh negotiation (https://www.softwaresecured.com/post/what-to-do-when-you-dont-agree-with-your-penetration-test-report). Retest/remediation-verification is a distinct, separately scoped follow-up engagement: it "normally validates specified original findings in the agreed environment; it is not automatically a new full penetration test" (https://onsecurity.io/article/how-to-evaluate-a-penetration-test-report/, https://essentialguide.docs.cisecurity.org/en/latest/bp/remediate_pen_test_findings.html).

## When the answer is ambiguous

Three distinct documented behaviors when the owner neither accepts nor refutes cleanly:

1. **Partial agreement, finding retained with adjusted rating.** Compliance-audit convention explicitly names this case: management "accepts the condition but disputes the cause or effect assessment," most often by asserting a compensating control; "the auditor retaining the finding but potentially modifying the risk rating" (https://auditingauthority.com/audit-findings-and-management-response/). The finding is not dropped merely because the owner contests part of it.

2. **Escalation to a named governance body when persuasion fails.** ISACA guidance: the auditor's first response to disagreement is to "elaborate and clarify the risks and exposures" — persuade, don't escalate immediately (https://www.briefmenow.org/isaca/during-an-exit-interview-in-cases-where-there-is-disagreement-regarding-the-impact-of-a-finding-an-is-auditor-should/). If that fails, the dispute goes to "senior leadership or the audit committee," which "may have to serve as the final authority in resolving disputes over audit findings" (synthesis grounded in ISACA guidance; corroborated qualitatively by https://www.richardchambers.com/when-the-audit-resolution-process-turns-into-a-food-fight/, which documents real-world audit disputes escalating past the auditor-auditee relationship). Separately, when a risk is accepted at a level *exceeding organizational risk appetite*, that acceptance itself must be escalated to the audit committee/board (grounded in the same ISACA-guidance synthesis, cross-referenced against https://internalauditor.theiia.org/en/articles/2022/february/risk-acceptance/, which independently confirms high-severity risk acceptance requires CEO-level approval, i.e. is never left at the auditee's own discretion).

3. **Publish with the disagreement noted.** No single source in this pass gives a named procedural label ("unresolved" as a report status), but the retained-finding-with-modified-rating convention above functionally *is* this behavior: the report is not blocked on agreement, and the finding, the auditor's position, and the auditee's rebuttal are recorded side by side. `[unsourced]` — an explicit "Unresolved" or "Disagreed" status field as a formal reporting convention (distinct from a modified risk rating) was not directly located and fetched in this pass; treat the practice as real (implied by the partial-agreement convention and by the "food fight" account of disputes that never fully resolve, https://www.richardchambers.com/when-the-audit-resolution-process-turns-into-a-food-fight/) but not confirmed as a named field.

**How professional standards treat a finding the auditee will not accept, specifically**: the sourced material converges on the same shape across IIA, ISACA-aligned, and compliance-audit practice — the auditor does *not* have unilateral authority to force acceptance or to withdraw the finding under pressure. The auditor's tools are (a) persuasion/clarification, (b) retaining the finding with a modified risk rating if partial technical ground is conceded, and (c) escalation to a governance body (audit committee/board) that holds final authority over disputed findings and over risk acceptances that exceed appetite. The auditor role stops at validate-and-escalate; it is never itself the adjudicator of its own contested findings (https://internalauditor.theiia.org/en/articles/2022/february/risk-acceptance/, ISACA-guidance synthesis above, https://auditingauthority.com/audit-findings-and-management-response/).

## What proceeds without asking

Consistent across every source above: nothing in the sourced material shows a reviewer stopping to ask a human before assigning an initial severity, before deciding whether an observation rises to the level of a reportable finding, or before choosing what/how much to sample. These are treated as the reviewer's own professional judgment calls, made solo, and only *surfaced* to a human if later contested:

- **Severity assignment**: the compliance-audit source shows severity being *re-negotiated after the fact* when management pushes back (https://auditingauthority.com/audit-findings-and-management-response/) — implying the initial assignment was the auditor's alone, not jointly set. Google's code-review pushback guide likewise treats the reviewer's initial call (block vs. nit) as unilateral, only escalating if the *author* pushes back (https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/pushback.html).
- **Whether something is a finding at all**: no sourced procedure requires the reviewer to check with the auditee before logging an observation as a finding; validation/confirmation (Section 1, moment 4) happens so the finding is *accurate*, not so it requires permission to exist.
- **Sampling choices**: not directly re-sourced here (see the companion report's AICPA material on statistical/non-statistical sampling, https://legalclarity.org/aicpa-audit-guide-key-concepts-in-audit-sampling/) — sample-size derivation is retained as an auditable record of the auditor's own methodology, not a decision put to the auditee.

This matters for the state machine: none of these should trigger a transition that stops to wait on a human. They're internal to `auditing`.

## Draft `user`-actor transitions

*(This section is this report's own synthesis, not sourced fact. It proposes how the moments above might map onto transitions in a state machine using the role's existing states idle/scoped/auditing/reported, with `user` as the human-in-the-loop actor.)*

| from | to | actor | precondition |
|---|---|---|---|
| idle | scoped | user | reviewer and auditee/sponsor have agreed engagement scope and boundaries (entrance-conference equivalent: Section 1 moments 1–2) |
| scoped | auditing | reviewer | scope accepted; no further human input required to begin |
| auditing | auditing | user | reviewer requests evidence/access the auditee must grant before a specific requirement can be checked (Section 1 moment 3) — stays in `auditing`, does not need its own state |
| auditing | auditing | user | reviewer needs to confirm a suspected finding with the owner before it is finalized (Section 1 moment 4) — a sub-loop within `auditing`, not a state transition |
| auditing | reported | reviewer | reviewer has completed its own severity/finding/sampling judgment calls (Section 4) without needing a human decision |
| reported | reported | user | owner disputes a finding; reviewer attempts resolution by clarification (Section 1 moment 5, Section 3 item 2) — recorded as a management-response artifact attached to the finding, not a new state |
| reported | reported | user | dispute escalates to a named governance actor (audit-committee/risk-owner equivalent) who adjudicates or the owner formally accepts residual risk (Section 1 moments 6 and 8) |

**Missing state named explicitly, not forced to fit**: none of the sourced material shows the disputed-finding, exit-conference, or risk-acceptance exchanges *reopening* `auditing` — they all operate on the already-`reported` finding set, which argues for keeping them as `reported`-internal loops rather than a new state, consistent with the companion report's conclusion that re-checks are better modeled as scoped re-entries than as a dedicated state. However, the exit-conference convention of *pre-circulating* a draft before the synchronous meeting (Section 2) suggests `reported` may need to distinguish a draft/pre-final sub-status from a final one if the state machine wants to model "draft shared, awaiting management response" separately from "final and closed" — this report does not have sourced evidence strong enough to justify minting a new top-level state for that distinction, only to flag it.

**On the verdict-set gap flagged by the companion report** (whether `Present`/`Surface`/`Absent`/`Incorrect` needs an `Unverifiable` case): this report's evidence-and-access material (Section 1 moment 3) supports it independently. The sourced material shows reviewers repeatedly needing to *request* evidence/access they do not have before a requirement can be checked at all (implicit across the audit-fieldwork and pentest-scoping sources above) — a case structurally distinct from `Absent` (verifiably not present) because the reviewer cannot verify presence or absence without access that was not granted or does not exist. This is the same gap the companion report names from a different angle (Fagan follow-up / AICPA tolerable-deviation framing); the interaction-side evidence here — that reviewers have a standing, repeated need to formally request access before proceeding — corroborates that `Unverifiable` should be a distinct verdict rather than folded into `Absent`.
